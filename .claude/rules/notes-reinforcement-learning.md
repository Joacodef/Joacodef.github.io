# Reinforcement Learning notes

What holds only for the notes of Prof. Rodrigo Toro's Reinforcement Learning course (IIC3675) at UC Chile, on top of `notes.md`. The figure modules, `gridworld.js` and `cartpole.js`, are described in `reinforcement-learning/figure-modules.md`, and each note's examples, runs and the numbers its page quotes in its file in `reinforcement-learning/`; each loads with its files. The scripts that compute those numbers are not in the repository; recompute them after any change.

## Sources

- The course README, `course-materials/reinforcement-learning/README.md`, has the syllabus and note status (with the sections still missing a figure), the notation and glossary tables, and how to read the PDFs; its `reference/chapters.md` says what each chapter file and each class's slides cover.
- The course follows Sutton and Barto, *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction* (2nd ed., 2020), and its readings are the book's chapters, saved in `course-materials/reinforcement-learning/`, grouped by exam rather than by chapter. The book is the reference for theory and formula notation. Its PDFs lose Greek letters and formulas in `pdftotext`, so render the pages with formulas to read them (the course README says how).
- The first exam's study guide and the class slides (in Spanish) give the sections each exam covers and the course's Spanish terms. Before fixing a note's scope, check the slides as well as the chapter file and tell Joaquín how far each goes. When the slides go further, as class 11 does into chapter 12 (up to tabular Sarsa(λ)), the note follows the slides' scope with the book's formulas. When the reading goes further, as chapter 9's file does past the class 13 slides (§9.5 and the first page of §9.6), the class's note keeps the slides' scope, and the sections only the reading covers get a note of their own, next in the same module (Joaquín's decision: feature construction, planned).
- Where a slide or the study guide differs from the book, the notes follow the book and point out the difference (the slides' trace e and their recap's R_t: see "Notation"). Slide 22 of class 13 calls the Monte Carlo, TD and n-step TD targets unbiased estimates of v_π, "al menos en el caso tabular"; the book says that bootstrapping targets depend on the weights and are biased, which is why those methods are semi-gradient.
- Beyond the book, the class 12 slides teach R-Max, which the book does not cover: for R-Max the slides are the reference, with their notation (see "Notation"). A pair is known once N(s, a) ≥ k; the slides' pseudocode writes > k, and the notes follow ≥ k. An unknown pair gives R_max and ends the episode, and value iteration runs on that model at every step. The original is Brafman and Tennenholtz, "R-max: a general polynomial time algorithm for near-optimal reinforcement learning" (JMLR 2002), which the model-based note names in its text.
- §9.6 warns that a step size like 1/t is not appropriate with function approximation; the prediction note's figures keep α fixed within an episode and shrink it from one episode to the next.
- Chapter 10's readings are §10.1–10.2 and §10.5, which relies on §10.3 (not in the files). Joaquín's decision: the control note (note 11) adds the part of §10.3 that §10.5 needs, and feature construction becomes note 12. When the class 14 slides arrive, compare them with the book.
- Never reuse the book's examples or the study guide's exercises: not the cliff, the windy gridworld, the random walk, driving home, blackjack, Jack's car rental, the gambler, tic-tac-toe, the maximization bias MDP with states A and B, the 10-armed testbed, or chapter 8's sum of a dozen dice, Dyna maze, blocking maze and shortcut maze, and not the exercises of the study guide (the sushi cart, the cave) or of the slides (in class 12, the grid where a key opens a door past a field of spikes, its table of rewards per 1,000 steps, the table of episode lengths on a maze, and the maze before and after an earthquake). Design each note's own worlds and numbers, and compute every number the page quotes with a script.
- Exception, decided by Joaquín: the interactive figures use the professor's 3 × 3 cookie grid and the cliff, the worlds he uses across several classes, so the figures look like what the class shows. The notes' text keeps its own examples, except where the cliff is introduced and compared: section 5 of the TD note, where the reader first meets it with the Sarsa figure, and the Q-learning note, whose text also uses it, so that text and figure support each other. The approximation notes use the class 13 cart-pole (the book's pole balancing, Example 3.4), the professor's example for prediction with approximation, in their text and figures, with no example of their own; the control note also uses its continuing version (−1 at each failure, then a fresh start), which the book gives for the same problem.

## The worlds

- **The cookie grid**, the professor's (class 8 slides): 3 × 3, start A1 (bottom left), cookie C3 (top right, +1, ends the episode), 0 for every other move, a move into a wall leaves the agent in place, γ = 0.9; under the random policy its values are 0.31 0.51 0 / 0.25 0.34 0.51 / 0.20 0.25 0.31, top row first.
- **The cliff** of the figures is 4 × 6, γ = 1; its optimal values and those of Sarsa's route are in `reinforcement-learning/q-learning.md`.
- The cart-pole is in `cartpole.js` (see `reinforcement-learning/figure-modules.md`).

## Interactive figures

Joaquín shaped and approved this design: build every new figure on it, on `gridworld.js`, starting from a mockup he approves.

- A figure is a guided run that the reader steps through like slides, with Next and Back, and each press is one line of the method's pseudocode as the note writes it: choose A (the chosen move turns yellow before the agent moves), take it, choose A′ when the method does, build the target or TD error, update. Each choice is explained once, where it happens: Sarsa-type methods choose A′ before updating, from the table as it stands, and Q-learning chooses after. The Monte Carlo figures play the episode with the table unchanged, the moves made (or, for state values, the cells left) outlined in dashed blue, then sweep back one step per press (G ← γG + R, then the average), turning each update green. Every step that updates shows that update in its readout, the value in green taking G, computed in red, or the average of its returns; a skipped visit shows only G.
- Every step of the first episode is played, never skipped, in every figure: the first moves go through every line, and the rest of episode 1 gets at least one press per move, combining lines (choosing and taking in one press, or a move with its TD error). A run's first episode must therefore be short, which decides its seed; on the cliff that is rare (one-step Sarsa's first episode has a median of 145 moves, and 413 of 60,000 seeds give 20 or fewer). On the cookie grid, whose episodes are short, a slide in episode 2 is also reached by playing that episode move by move (and its sweep step by step), not by a skip of a few moves into its middle, which looks odd.
- From episode 2 on, slides skip to the moments where something new is learned, playing the skipped moves quickly and saying how many were skipped, and may combine lines. A skip plays the motion of the episode it lands on, not the last states of the episodes it passes, and a slide's words match what its skip shows (Joaquín's reviews of the control note). In the frames of a skip, a wall hit shows the agent pressed against the wall every other frame, so that a run of hits shakes it there, and a skip over a run of hits says so in its note. The last slide adds Reset.
- A small figure about a single decision can instead be something the reader drags, as in the computer vision notes: the model-based note's corridor figure, in section 2, moves the untried door.
- A slide claims only what holds in its run at every move, not only at the slides shown. A figure that claims something about the rest of its run checks it in code and warns in the console when it fails.
- The grid: each cell the agent can stand on is split by its diagonals into four triangles, one per move, each showing that move's value (blank while untried, unless a target uses it), with the best value of the cell in bold. Marks on the triangles: yellow (`--pick`) for the move just chosen, which fades while the agent takes it, green (`--ok`) for moves being updated (as strong as their trace, in Sarsa(λ)), red for the move or moves a target uses, and dashed blue for the steps of an n-step window. There is no cell selection, and no arrow on the move being made: the agent's movement shows each move (Joaquín found such arrows unattractive and cell selection redundant). The one arrow is the best move's: over the heatmap, a small blue triangle at the middle of a cell points toward it (Joaquín's idea: a strongly filled triangle points toward the middle, against its own move, and looked like an arrow the wrong way).
- A slide about the route the best moves trace (a first way through, the shortest path, the end of the run) draws that route as a light blue band from the start to the goal, under the values, ending in an arrowhead, and the values it runs over sit on the band's color (it replaced a thin line, which struck through the numbers). Every figure on a grid ends with such a band: the cookie grid figures that estimate state values draw the route their estimates lead along.
- The heatmap (decided with Joaquín after testing several scales): under the lines, each value shown fills its triangle (by move, the default) or each cell takes its best value (by cell; a figure with one value per cell fills the cell either way), green (`--ok`) as a positive value nears 1, by its square so that 0.73, 0.81, 0.9 and 1 stay apart, and coral (`--coral`) as a negative one nears −10, the deepest from there on; 0 is not shaded, so a fresh grid stays clean. Green is mixed into the background up to `--heat-max`, and coral up to 0.4 of that, so that costs recede: a strong color reads as the move to pick, and Joaquín found that a strong purple, deepest at the cliff's falls and far corner, looked like a preferred choice. A scale that turned the cliff's values greener as they neared 0 would not work either: in each cliff figure's first episode, the moves tried once into a wall (−0.5, −0.75) would be the greenest on the grid while the agent picks among the blank untried ones, worth 0. R-Max's untried moves keep their gray shade and take no color until known. With the heatmap off, the figures are drawn exactly as before it existed.
- Settings: each figure has a round button with a gear at the end of its controls, after Next (Reset appears before it), that opens a menu above it for that figure only: the heatmap (by move, by cell, off) and the sound (on, off). A reader's choices are kept as the start for the next figures and pages; Escape closes the menu, and the arrow keys inside it do not change slides. The button sits in the controls rather than on the grid, where it would cover values and the cookie's +1.
- A figure with two runs, such as the batch figure's batch TD(0) and batch Monte Carlo, or the wall figure's R-Max and Dyna-Q, picks one with `.modes` buttons above the drawing.
- The grid, the marks and the presses are explained once, in the lead-in of the first figure a reader meets: the Monte Carlo prediction figure in section 1 of the Monte Carlo note, on the cookie grid, with one value per cell; its lead-in also introduces the heatmap's green and the settings button. The control figure in section 4 adds the values of moves, in triangles, with the best one in bold and the blue triangle that points to it, and the heatmap by cell. The TD(0) figure in section 1 of the TD note learns from the same 30 episodes as the prediction figure (the same seed) and introduces the red mark, the batch figure in section 4 replays them and introduces passes and the mode buttons, the Sarsa figure in section 5 introduces the cliff and the heatmap's coral for negative values, and the later figures' lead-ins and first slides say only what is new (Q-learning's choice after the update and its maximum, the n-step window, the traces; in the model-based note, the faint copy in section 1, R-Max's gray shade in section 2, the model's empty start in section 3) and link back to it.
- The controls sit right under the grid, with Back and Next first, so that they never move as the text grows: readers press Next quickly. New text appears only below the old, which dims. While the grid animates, Back and Next are frozen, so that quick presses cannot pile one animation on another: during a skip they look disabled (`aria-disabled`, which keeps the focus on them, so that the arrow keys work again when it ends), and after a press they ignore presses for about half a second, until the move has played out; with reduced motion nothing animates, and nothing is frozen.
- Sounds made in the browser (a click for a move, a duller one for a wall, a falling tone for a fall, a soft crunch when the agent eats the cookie, a chime at the end) play only after Next or the right arrow key, and the figure's settings turn them off.
- Figures must stay light on any reader's device. The run behind a figure is small and computed once, from a fixed seed, so every reader sees the same run: the cliff's 30 episodes are about 650 to 900 moves and take a few milliseconds when the page loads. Animations play only after a press, last at most about two seconds and stop by themselves; with reduced motion they are replaced by an immediate change. Nothing runs in the background.
- The cart-pole figures have no heatmap (their settings offer the sound alone) and end without a route band, since the cart-pole has no route.
- What the agent learned must read at once: draw learned values over pictures of the situations they belong to (bars, each over a small drawing of its situation, on an axis named in plain words), with few labeled quantities, prefer features or groupings the reader can picture, keep a run's noise from hiding the result (the prediction note shrinks its step size so that the bars settle), and make the last slide say in plain words what was learned. Keep one running example per note when the class's example can carry it: a second toy example splits the reader's attention.
- Check every figure at 320 px for readout equations wider than their box (a build's `fit` sets a long one smaller on phones). After changing `gridworld.js` or `cartpole.js`, compare each figure press by press with the live site or the mockup, drawing and text, to check that nothing else moved.

## Notation

- The book's symbols, as the course README's notation table lists them: `S_t`, `A_t`, `R_{t+1}`, `G_t`, `γ`, `π`, `b`, `v_π`, `q_π`, `q_*`, `V`, `Q`, `α`, `ε`, `G_{t:t+n}`, `ρ_{t:h}`, `V̄`, for chapter 12 `λ`, `G_t^λ` and `z_t`, and for chapter 8 `p(s′, r | s, a)` (the true dynamics), `Model(S, A)` and `n` (planning steps). R-Max uses the slides' `N(s, a)` and `N(s, a, s′, r)` (the pseudocode writes N_t and N_p), the estimate `P(s′, r | s, a)` = N(s, a, s′, r)/N(s, a) (capital P, against the book's p for the true dynamics), `k` and `R_max` (written `<i>R</i><sub>max</sub>`); the value of a move under R-Max's model is written `Q(s, a)`, and value iteration's update uses `V`. Chapter 9 adds the feature vector x(s) with d components x_i(s), bold like w (`<b class="vec">x</b>`), the linear estimate v̂(s, w) = wᵀx(s), the mean squared value error VE(w) with its bar (`<span class="ovl">VE</span>`, in the running text and the readouts too), the on-policy distribution μ(s) with η(s) and h(s), the target U_t of the general update, and, for linear TD, b and A, bold, and the TD fixed point w_TD = A⁻¹b. Chapter 10 adds q̂(s, a, w) = wᵀx(s, a) with features of a state and an action, the greedy action A*_{t+1} = argmax_a q̂(S_{t+1}, a, w_t) (written with `.ss`), the average reward r(π), its estimate R̄ (`<span class="ovl"><i>R</i></span>`, in readouts and status lines too; never a combining macron, which sits beside the letter), the differential return G_t and the step size β of R̄.
- In HTML, write time indices as subscripts with italic letters (`<i>S</i><sub><i>t</i>+1</sub>`), the optimal values as `<i>q</i><sub>*</sub>`, the update arrow as `←`, and a maximum as `max<sub><i>a</i></sub>`. Write state and action names upright: `<i>Q</i>(start, bridge)`. Stack a superscript over a subscript with `.ss` (`<i>G</i><span class="ss"><span>λ</span><span><i>t</i></span></span>` for G_t^λ), the limits of a sum or product with `.lims`, an expected value's bar with `.ovl` (V̄), and the book's bold vectors with `<b class="vec">w</b>`. The eligibility trace is z, as in the book's 2nd edition; the notes mention that it is also written e, as in the slides. The reward after A_t is R_{t+1}, as in the book, although the slides' recap writes R_t.
- In code snippets, name variables after the symbols: `q`, `q1`, `q2`, `alpha`, `gamma`, `eps`, `pi`, `g` (a return or a target), `rho`, `z`, `lam` (λ; `lambda` is a Python keyword), `s_next`, `a_next`.
- Spanish terms: the glossary in the course README, which also lists the terms the Spanish notes keep in English, as the slides do.

## Ink

- Blue ink (`.ln`, `.ln-path`): routes, the paths an agent takes through a world, and the transitions they are made of, including those a model holds, and in the grid figures the triangle that points to a cell's best move, the move a greedy route takes. In a diagram that sets a model against the world (section 4 of the model-based note), blue is what the model says and ink the world: a door the world has locked is an ink wall (`.wall-line`), one only the model believes locked a blue wall (`.wall-line.model`), and a way the world has open that the model does not know dashed ink (`.ink-dash`). Where an untried move leads is a dashed blue outline (`.state.unk`).
- Carmine (`.pt`, `.pt-dot`): the target of an update, the value an estimate moves toward, and the TD error that carries it back. In formulas, the target is wrapped in `.pt`, in the Monte Carlo note too, where it is the return G (its legend says so, and its figures' readouts show G red where it is computed and averaged in); in backup diagrams, the nodes the target is built from are carmine; in timelines, the estimate a target bootstraps from.
- In the grid figures, yellow (`--pick`) marks the move an agent has just chosen, and green (`--ok`) a value being updated. Green is also the heatmap's fill of a high value, the outline drawn over the fill (decided with Joaquín); the heatmap's coral is a pale red toward orange, so that a cost's fill stays apart from the strong carmine outline of a target. In a figure's readout, the value being updated, the left side of the update's arrow, is green (`.upd`), in the presses whose grid outlines it in green, as the target is red like its red outline.
- In the cart-pole figures, the estimates are blue bars, one per range of the pole's angle, and what they aim for is red: the true values of visited states as dots, each range's long-run average as a short line. The push just chosen is yellow, and the range being updated and the range a target borrows from are outlined in green and red, as in the grid figures. In the control note the bars are values of pushes, the same blue; the push worth more in a situation is a small blue arrow beside its cart (blue for the preferred move, as the grid's best-move triangle), and on the big cart in Figure 1. A learning curve is a blue line over the faint ink of the others.
- Everything else, including states, the pair being updated and the edges of backup diagrams, is drawn in ink.
- Backup diagrams follow the book: an open circle is a state, a small filled dot an action, and an arc across branches a maximum. The pair being updated is at the top.
