# Reinforcement Learning notes

What holds only for the notes of Prof. Rodrigo Toro's Reinforcement Learning course (IIC3675) at UC Chile, on top of `notes.md`.

## Sources

- The course README, `course-materials/reinforcement-learning/README.md`, has the syllabus and note status (with the sections still missing a figure), the notation and glossary tables, and how to read the PDFs.
- The course follows Sutton and Barto, *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction* (2nd ed., 2020), and its readings are the book's chapters. They are saved in `course-materials/reinforcement-learning/`, grouped by exam rather than by chapter. The book is the reference for theory and formula notation. Its PDFs lose Greek letters and formulas in `pdftotext`, so render the pages with formulas to read them (the course README says how).
- The first exam's study guide and the class slides (in Spanish) give the sections each exam covers and the course's Spanish terms. When the slides cover more of a chapter than its PDF, as class 11 does for chapter 12 (up to tabular Sarsa(λ)), the note follows the slides' scope with the book's formulas.
- Never reuse the book's examples or the study guide's exercises: not the cliff, the windy gridworld, the random walk, driving home, blackjack, Jack's car rental, the gambler, tic-tac-toe, the maximization bias MDP with states A and B, or the 10-armed testbed, and not the exercises of the study guide (the sushi cart, the cave) or of the slides. Design each note's own worlds and numbers, and compute every number the page quotes with a script.
- Exception, decided by Joaquín on 29 Sep 2026: the interactive figures use the professor's 3 × 3 cookie grid and the cliff, the worlds he uses across several classes, so the figures look like what the class shows. The notes' text keeps its own examples, except where the cliff is introduced and compared: section 5 of the TD note, where the reader first meets it with the Sarsa figure, and the Q-learning note, whose text also uses it (decided the same day): its own example had been a smaller cliff, and one world lets the text and the figure support each other.

## Interactive figures

- A figure is a guided run that the reader steps through like slides, with Next and Back, and each press is one line of the method's pseudocode as the note writes it: choose A (the chosen move turns yellow before the agent moves), take it, choose A′ when the method does, build the target or TD error, update. Each choice is explained once, where it happens: Sarsa-type methods choose A′ before updating, from the table as it stands, and Q-learning chooses after. The Monte Carlo figures play the episode with the table unchanged, the moves made (or, for state values, the cells left) outlined in dashed blue, then sweep back one step per press (G ← γG + R, then the average), turning each update green. Every step that updates shows that update in its readout, the value in green taking G, computed in red, or the average of its returns; a skipped visit shows only G (asked by Joaquín on 2 Oct 2026). TD(0)'s figure predicts values for a fixed random policy: choose (the policy's pick, in yellow), take, the TD error with its target, update; after move 1, choosing and taking share a press. Every step of the first episode is played, never skipped, in every figure (asked by Joaquín on 1 Oct 2026): the first moves go through every line, and the rest of episode 1 gets at least one press per move, combining lines (choosing and taking in one press, or a move with its TD error). A run's first episode must therefore be short, which decides its seed; on the cliff that is rare (one-step Sarsa's first episode has a median of 145 moves, and 413 of 60,000 seeds give 20 or fewer). On the cookie grid, whose episodes are short, a slide in episode 2 is also reached by playing that episode move by move (and its sweep step by step), not by a skip of a few moves into its middle, which looks odd (Joaquín, the same day). From episode 2 on, slides skip to the moments where something new is learned, playing the skipped moves quickly and saying how many were skipped, and may combine lines. The last slide adds Reset. Each new figure still starts with a mockup.
- The grid: each cell the agent can stand on is split by its diagonals into four triangles, one per move, each showing that move's value (blank while untried; on the cliff, at most one decimal on phones, while the cookie grid's larger cells keep two), with the best value of the cell on a blue highlight. Marks on the triangles: yellow (`--pick`) for the move just chosen, which fades while the agent takes it (`taken` in a build; asked by Joaquín on 2 Oct 2026), green (`--ok`) for moves being updated (as strong as their trace, in Sarsa(λ)), red for the move or moves a target uses, and dashed blue for the steps of an n-step window. There are no arrows on moves and no cell selection: the agent's movement shows each move. A slide about the route the best moves trace (a first way through, the shortest path, the end of the run) draws that route as a light blue band from the start to the goal, under the values, ending in an arrowhead, and the values it runs over sit on the band's color (`route` in a build; asked for by Joaquín on 1 Oct 2026, after a thin line struck through the numbers). Every figure ends with such a band: the cookie grid figures that estimate state values draw the route their estimates lead along (`valuePath`: in each cell, the move with the largest reward plus γ times the next cell's value), and a value the band runs over sits on a chip of the band's color (asked by Joaquín on 2 Oct 2026). The agent, walls and falls are ink. On the cookie grid the goal is a drawn cookie with a bite out of it (dough and chips mixed from the color tokens, light dough and dark chips in both themes), with +1 beside it; the agent eats it, so it is gone while the agent stands on the goal (shrinking and fading after a press) and back for the next episode. The sides of the cells are a shade stronger than the diagonals inside them. A move with marks of two kinds (a pair updated toward a target that uses its own value) shows both, the later one inside the earlier. A figure that estimates state values (`perCell: true` in `slideshow()`, with `V` in place of `Q`) draws no diagonals and one value per cell, above the agent; its green and red marks outline the whole cell, and only the move just chosen is still a triangle, in yellow.
- The grid, the marks and the presses are explained once, in the lead-in of the first figure a reader meets: the Monte Carlo prediction figure in section 1 of the Monte Carlo note, on the cookie grid, with one value per cell. The control figure in section 4 adds the values of moves, in triangles, with the best one highlighted. The TD(0) figure in section 1 of the TD note learns from the same 30 episodes as the prediction figure (the same seed) and introduces the red mark, the Sarsa figure in section 5 introduces the cliff, and the later figures' lead-ins and first slides say only what is new (Q-learning's choice after the update and its maximum, the n-step window, the traces) and link back to it.
- A slide claims only what holds in its run at every move, not only at the slides shown. One-step Sarsa with α = 0.5 does not settle on a route within 30 episodes (8 of 60,000 seeds do by episode 22), since its target uses the random next move and the open cells' values stay within a few tenths of each other; its figure builds on what does hold, a fall picked as A′ pushing Q(A2, right) below Q(A2, up) for good.
- The controls sit right under the grid, with Back and Next first, so that they never move as the text grows: readers press Next quickly. New text appears only below the old, which dims.
- The figures share `src/assets/js/notes/reinforcement-learning/gridworld.js`: the two worlds, `cookie` and `cliff`, built by one function (each with its own cell names, rewards, texts for choosing and taking a move, and cell size), the grid, the sounds and the slide controls. Each figure's script computes its own run and slides and hands them to `slideshow()`, with the cookie grid as `world` when it is not the cliff: `monte-carlo.js`, `td-learning.js` (TD(0) and Sarsa), `q-learning.js`, `n-step-bootstrapping.js` and `eligibility-traces.js`. After changing the module, compare each figure press by press with the live site, drawing and text, to check that nothing else moved.
- Sounds made in the browser (a click for a move, a duller one for a wall, a falling tone for a fall, a soft crunch when the agent eats the cookie, a chime at the end) play only after Next or the right arrow key, and a button turns them off.
- Figures must stay light on any reader's device. The run behind a figure is small and computed once, from a fixed seed, so every reader sees the same run: the cliff's 30 episodes are about 800 to 900 moves and take a few milliseconds when the page loads. Animations play only after a press, last at most about two seconds and stop by themselves; with reduced motion they are replaced by an immediate change. Nothing runs in the background.

## Notation

- The book's symbols, as the course README's notation table lists them: `S_t`, `A_t`, `R_{t+1}`, `G_t`, `γ`, `π`, `b`, `v_π`, `q_π`, `q_*`, `V`, `Q`, `α`, `ε`, `G_{t:t+n}`, `ρ_{t:h}`, `V̄`, and for chapter 12 `λ`, `G_t^λ` and `z_t`.
- In HTML, write time indices as subscripts with italic letters (`<i>S</i><sub><i>t</i>+1</sub>`), the optimal values as `<i>q</i><sub>*</sub>`, the update arrow as `←`, and a maximum as `max<sub><i>a</i></sub>`. Write state and action names upright: `<i>Q</i>(start, bridge)`. Stack a superscript over a subscript with `.ss` (`<i>G</i><span class="ss"><span>λ</span><span><i>t</i></span></span>` for G_t^λ), the limits of a sum or product with `.lims`, an expected value's bar with `.ovl` (V̄), and the book's bold vectors with `<b class="vec">w</b>`. The eligibility trace is z, as in the book's 2nd edition; the notes mention that it is also written e, as in the slides.
- In code snippets, name variables after the symbols: `q`, `q1`, `q2`, `alpha`, `gamma`, `eps`, `pi`, `g` (a return or a target), `rho`, `z`, `lam` (λ; `lambda` is a Python keyword), `s_next`, `a_next`.
- Spanish terms come from the study guide and the slides, which keep many terms in English: greedy, ε-greedy, bandit, Sarsa, Q-values, value iteration, first-visit, every-visit, baseline, n-step TD, n-step Sarsa, 4-step Sarsa, TD error, eligibility traces, λ-return. The Spanish notes keep those in English too, as well as Q-learning, Expected Sarsa, Double Q-learning, on-policy, off-policy, bootstrapping, afterstates, forward view and backward view (glossed as vista hacia adelante and hacia atrás). The rest follows the glossary in the course README: retorno, retorno de n pasos, recompensa, política, política de comportamiento, política objetivo, objetivo (of an update), estado terminal, episodio, tamaño de paso α, descuento γ, sesgo de maximización, razón de muestreo por importancia.

## Ink

- Blue ink (`.ln`, `.ln-path`): routes, the paths an agent takes through a world, and the transitions they are made of.
- Carmine (`.pt`, `.pt-dot`): the target of an update, the value an estimate moves toward, and the TD error that carries it back. In formulas, the target is wrapped in `.pt`, in the Monte Carlo note too, where it is the return G (its legend says so, and its figures' readouts show G red where it is computed and averaged in; asked by Joaquín on 2 Oct 2026); in backup diagrams, the nodes the target is built from are carmine; in timelines, the estimate a target bootstraps from.
- In the grid figures, yellow (`--pick`) marks the move an agent has just chosen, and green (`--ok`) a value being updated. In a figure's readout, the value being updated, the left side of the update's arrow, is green (`.upd`), in the presses whose grid outlines it in green, as the target is red like its red outline (asked by Joaquín on 2 Oct 2026).
- Everything else, including states, the pair being updated and the edges of backup diagrams, is drawn in ink.
- Backup diagrams follow the book: an open circle is a state, a small filled dot an action, and an arc across branches a maximum. The pair being updated is at the top.

## The notes' own examples

- **Monte Carlo and TD: the wandering home.** Kitchen, hallway, study and charger (terminal), γ = 1, −1 per move. The robot's random policy: kitchen → hallway always; hallway → study or kitchen, ½ each; study → charger or hallway, ½ each. Its values are −9, −8 and −5; q_π(study, charger) = −1, q_π(study, hallway) = −9, q_π(hallway, study) = −6, q_π(hallway, kitchen) = −10. Two trips, kitchen-hallway-study and kitchen-hallway-kitchen-hallway-study-hallway-study, give first-visit estimates −5, −4, −2 and every-visit −5, −3.5, −1.67; after 10,000 simulated trips both are within 0.1 of the true values. The greedy policy is optimal (−3, −2, −1); ε-greedy around it with ε = 0.2 has values −3.47, −2.47, −1.25; in the study, Σπ′q_π = 0.9(−1) + 0.1(−9) = −1.8 ≥ −5. TD(0) with α = 0.5 from −5, −4, −2 on the trip kitchen-hallway-kitchen-hallway-study-charger: TD errors 0, −2, −1, 2, 1, table −5.5, −4, −1.5 (constant-α Monte Carlo: −4, −3, −1.5); with the table fixed the TD errors are 0, −2, 0, 1, 1 and add up to G_0 − V(kitchen) = 0. Batch example: hallway −1 → study once (charger taken, 0), study +1 three times and 0 once: V(study) = 0.6 for both, V(hallway) = −1 for batch Monte Carlo and −0.4 for batch TD(0). Sarsa escaping the shuttle: Q(hallway, kitchen) = 0.5(−1 + 0 − 0) = −0.5.
