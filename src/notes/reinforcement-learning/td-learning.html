Monte Carlo methods learn nothing from an episode until it is over, since only then is the return known. Yet much can be learned before that. Picture the robot of the previous note, wandering through a home with a kitchen, a hallway, a study and a charger, at a cost of 1 per move. When it steps from the hallway back into the kitchen, it already knows its trip just got longer. It need not reach the charger to lower its estimate of the hallway.

Temporal-difference (TD) learning updates an estimate right after each step, toward the reward just received plus the current estimate of the state that step led to. It learns a guess from a guess. That turns out to work well: TD methods need no model of the world, learn while the episode is still running, and often learn faster than Monte Carlo methods. This note covers TD for predicting values, what it converges to, and Sarsa, the same idea for learning to act.

Throughout this page, the steps of an episode are drawn in blue ink, and the target of an update, and the estimate it relies on, in red ink.

1Learning from the next step

Constant-α Monte Carlo moves the estimate of each visited state toward the return that followed it:

V(St) ← V(St) + α[Gt − V(St)]

but Gt is known only when the episode ends. The simplest TD method, TD(0) or one-step TD, uses instead the reward Rt+1 and the estimate of the next state, both available at time t + 1:

V(St) ← V(St) + α[Rt+1 + γV(St+1) − V(St)]

Why is that a sensible target? The return of one step is the next reward plus the discounted return of the next step, Gt = Rt+1 + γGt+1, so the value of a state can be written in two ways:

vπ(s) = Eπ[Gt | St = s] = Eπ[Rt+1 + γvπ(St+1) | St = s]

Monte Carlo estimates the first form: it replaces the expectation by a sampled return. Dynamic programming uses the second, computing the expectation exactly from a model of the world but relying on the current estimate V for vπ. TD does both at once: it samples the second form, with one real reward and next state, and uses V(St+1) for vπ(St+1). Like dynamic programming, it bootstraps; like Monte Carlo, it needs no model.

The bracket measures how wrong the estimate of St turned out to be, one step later. It is the TD error:

δt ≐ Rt+1 + γV(St+1) − V(St)

The algorithm

Tabular TD(0), for a policy π and a step size α in (0, 1]:

  1. Start with any V(s), except V(terminal) = 0.
  2. At the start of each episode, observe the first state S.
  3. Take the action π gives for S, and observe the reward R and the next state S′.
  4. Update V(S) ← V(S) + α[R + γV(S′) − V(S)].
  5. Set S ← S′, and go back to step 3 until S is terminal.

The figure runs TD(0) on the same 30 episodes as the first figure of the previous note: the random moves are the same, and only the learning differs. γ = 0.9 again, α = 0.5, and every estimate starts at 0. Every move is now followed by its TD error, whose target uses the estimate outlined in red, and by the update.

2One trip, step by step

Say the robot's estimates are V(kitchen) = −5, V(hallway) = −4 and V(study) = −2, averages of two lucky trips; the true values of its wandering are −9, −8 and −5. Its next trip goes kitchen, hallway, kitchen, hallway, study, charger. With γ = 1 and α = 0.5, TD(0) makes one update per move:

tMoveTD errorUpdate
0kitchen → hallway−1 − 4 + 5 = 0none
1hallway → kitchen−1 − 5 + 4 = −2V(hallway): −4 → −5
2kitchen → hallway−1 − 5 + 5 = −1V(kitchen): −5 → −5.5
3hallway → study−1 − 2 + 5 = 2V(hallway): −5 → −4
4study → charger−1 + 0 + 2 = 1V(study): −2 → −1.5

At t = 1, stepping back into the kitchen tells the robot that the hallway was worse than it thought, and TD lowers the hallway's estimate on the spot. At t = 3 the robot reaches the study from the hallway, which is better news, and the estimate goes back up. Constant-α Monte Carlo waits until the charger, then moves each visit's estimate toward its return: the kitchen toward −5 and then −3, ending at −4, the hallway toward −4 and −2, ending at −3, and the study toward −1, ending at −1.5.

The Monte Carlo error as a sum of TD errors

If the table does not change during the episode, the error of the Monte Carlo update is the discounted sum of the TD errors along the rest of the episode, because the estimates in between cancel in pairs:

Gt − V(St) = δt + γδt+1 + ⋯ = ΣT−1k=tγk−tδk

With the table held at −5, −4 and −2, the TD errors of this trip are 0, −2, 0, 1 and 1, which add up to 0: exactly G0 − V(kitchen) = −5 − (−5). TD(0) changes the table as it goes, so the identity then holds only approximately; the third TD error above is −1 instead of 0 because the hallway had already been updated.

3Why learn from a guess?

Compared with dynamic programming, TD needs no model: no probabilities of rewards and next states, only experience. Compared with Monte Carlo, it learns online, one step at a time. That matters when episodes are long, when the task never ends, and when some policies would make an episode last forever: Monte Carlo can learn nothing from an episode that never ends, while TD notices during the episode that things are going badly.

It is also sound. For a fixed policy π, TD(0) converges to vπ: in the mean for a small enough constant α, and with probability 1 if α shrinks in the usual way, with a sum that grows without limit and a sum of squares that stays finite. Which method learns faster has not been proved in general, but in practice TD usually beats constant-α Monte Carlo on tasks with randomness.

4Batch updating: what each method converges to

With a fixed amount of experience, say a few episodes, one can replay it until the estimates stop changing. In batch updating, the increments of every step of every episode are computed and added up, the table changes once by their sum, and the whole batch is processed again, until it converges. With a small enough α, batch TD(0) converges to a single answer that does not depend on α, and so does batch Monte Carlo, but the two answers differ.

An example. The charger is sometimes taken by another robot. Reaching it from the study gives +1 if it is free and 0 if it is taken, and moving from the hallway to the study gives −1; γ = 1. Five episodes were recorded:

EpisodesWhat happened
1hallway, −1, study, 0 (the charger was taken)
3study, +1
1study, 0

Both methods give the study the average of its five returns, 0, 1, 1, 1 and 0: V(study) = 0.6. They disagree about the hallway. Batch Monte Carlo averages the hallway's only return, V(hallway) = −1 + 0 = −1. Batch TD(0) says the hallway always led to the study with a reward of −1, so V(hallway) = −1 + 0.6 = −0.4.

Batch Monte Carlo finds the estimates with the smallest squared error on the data seen: −1 fits the hallway's only return perfectly. Batch TD(0) finds the estimates that are exactly right for the maximum-likelihood model of the process, built from the data: each transition's probability is the fraction of times it was observed, and its reward is the average reward observed. This is the certainty-equivalence estimate, the answer one would get by treating the estimated model as certain. If the process is Markov, the TD answer should predict future returns better: the one bad outcome after the hallway was the charger's doing, and five visits to the study say more about the charger than one.

This helps explain why TD tends to learn faster. Computing the certainty-equivalence estimate directly is rarely practical: with n states, the model alone can need memory of order n2, and solving it about n3 steps. TD approaches the same answer with memory of order n, and online TD, which does not replay, still moves roughly toward it.

5Sarsa: TD for control

To learn to act without a model, TD learns action values, and the policy improves along the way: evaluate with TD, act ε-greedily with respect to the current estimates, and repeat, as in generalized policy iteration. The update is the same as TD(0), from one state–action pair to the next:

Q(St, At) ← Q(St, At) + α[Rt+1 + γQ(St+1, At+1) − Q(St, At)]

It is made after every transition from a nonterminal state, with Q(terminal, ·) = 0. It uses the five quantities St, At, Rt+1, St+1 and At+1, which give the method its name. Sarsa is on-policy: At+1 is the action the policy actually takes next, so Sarsa learns the values of the policy it follows, exploration included.

Sarsa converges with probability 1 to an optimal policy and its action values if every pair keeps being visited, the step sizes shrink in the usual way, and the policy becomes greedy in the limit, for example ε-greedy with ε = 1/t.

The algorithm

For a step size α in (0, 1] and a small ε > 0, with Q(s, a) arbitrary except Q(terminal, ·) = 0:

  1. At the start of each episode, observe S, and choose A ε-greedily with respect to Q.
  2. Take A, and observe R and S′. Choose A′ in S′ ε-greedily with respect to Q.
  3. Update Q(S, A) ← Q(S, A) + α[R + γQ(S′, A′) − Q(S, A)].
  4. Set S ← S′ and A ← A′, and go back to step 2 until S is terminal.

Learning within the episode is what lets Sarsa escape a policy that would never finish. Suppose all of the robot's action values start at 0 and, breaking the tie, its policy sends it from the hallway back to the kitchen, from where the only move is back to the hallway. Followed greedily, that policy shuttles forever, and a Monte Carlo method would wait forever for a return. With Sarsa, α = 0.5 and γ = 1, the first time the robot goes back to the kitchen, Q(hallway, kitchen) drops to 0.5(−1 + 0 − 0) = −0.5, below the 0 of the untried move to the study, and the next time it is in the hallway it heads for the study.

Sarsa's target uses the move the agent actually makes next, exploring moves included, so its values count what exploring costs. Next to the edge, every step has a 0.025 chance of a random move down into the cliff; one row up, a random move down only lands on the edge. That makes the edge worth less to Sarsa. In the figure the values still jump with every fall, since α = 0.5 moves an estimate halfway to each target. With a small step size and many episodes, they settle at the values of the ε-greedy policy the agent follows, which average in every fall: in A2, moving up is worth qπ(A2, up) = −9.39, and moving right, onto the edge, qπ(A2, right) = −12.01. The route one row up, 9 moves, is the best an agent can do while it keeps exploring. It is not the best route: once the exploring stops, the edge is two moves shorter and never falls.
