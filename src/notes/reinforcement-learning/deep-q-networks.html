Every agent of the earlier notes was handed its state: a cell of a grid, or the cart-pole's angle and swing, features someone chose. The paper this note follows, by Mnih and six colleagues at DeepMind (2013), gives an agent only what a person at the console has: the screen of an Atari 2600 game, the score, the signal that the game is over, and the controls. With the same network and the same way of learning for every game, it learned to play seven games, played better than every earlier method on six of them, and better than an expert human on three.

The figures follow the paper on a game of this note's own, small enough to watch every step of its learning. On a screen of 8 by 6 pixels, a ball bounces off the top, bottom and left walls, and the agent moves a paddle on the right edge up or down, or keeps it still, to hit the ball back. A hit is worth 1 point, and a miss costs 1 and ends the game. Section 1 gives the rest of the rules.

Networks with many layers, deep learning, had learned to recognize images from many examples, each labeled with its answer and independent of the others. A game offers neither, and the paper names three obstacles:

  • The point comes screens later

  • Each screen nearly repeats the last

  • How it plays decides what it sees

The score changes only when the ball reaches the paddle, a few screens after the move that put the paddle there, and nothing says which move earned it. One screen differs from the next by a pixel or two, so screens taken in order teach nearly the same thing many times. And what the agent sees depends on how it plays: a paddle kept near the top fills its experience with screens of the paddle near the top, and the mix shifts whenever its play does. The paper meets the first obstacle with Q-learning, whose targets carry each reward back to the moves before it, and the other two with a memory of its own play, from which it learns in random order.

Throughout this page, the network's estimates are drawn in blue ink, and what they aim for, the target of an update or the exact value of a move, in red ink. The move just chosen is yellow.

1Values from the screen

The paper's agent plays through an emulator of the console, ℰ. At each time step t it picks an action at from 𝒜 = {1, …, K}, here K = 3: up, stay and down; it sees an image xt ∈ ℝd, the pixels of the screen (here d = 48, each 0 or 1); and it receives a reward rt, the change in the score.

Within a step the paddle moves first, then the ball, then the check: when the ball arrives beside the paddle's column, it is hit if the paddle covers its row, and turns back, or else missed. Each episode starts with a serve from the left wall, on any of the 6 rows, rising or falling, so there are 12 serves, with the paddle on rows 2 and 3. A rally that reaches 10 hits is cut there: the cut is this game's own rule, not an ending, and the agent learns as if play went on.

One screen does not show the whole situation: a ball beside the paddle may be rising or falling, and the move that hits it depends on which. The paper calls such states perceptually aliased, different situations that one screen shows alike, and takes as the state the whole sequence of screens and actions so far,

st = x1, a1, x2, …, at−1, xt

A state should hold everything that decides what happens next, given the move, and the sequence does. In the paper's games every episode ends, so there are finitely many sequences, each a state of its own: a large but finite problem, to which the earlier notes' methods apply.

A network needs an input of fixed length, while a sequence grows, so the paper feeds it φ(st), the last four screens, each shrunk to a gray image of 84 × 84 pixels. In this note's game the ball moves one pixel diagonally per step, in a straight line between bounces, so two screens already show where it is going, as the swing told which way the pole moved in the control note:

φ(st) = (xt−1, xt)

At the serve, the first screen counts twice.

The agent aims at the return, the rewards from t on, each discounted by γ for every step it comes later (the paper gives no value; the figures use γ = 0.9):

Rt = ΣTt′=tγt′−trt′

and the value of an action is the largest expected return after it, over all ways of playing on,

Q*(s, a) = maxπ E[Rt | st = s, at = a, π]

For this game the page can compute Q* exactly, by the value iteration of the model-based note, over the game's true state: where the ball is, which way it moves, and where the paddle is, 552 situations in all. The agent can do nothing of the kind: it knows neither the rules nor the state, only screens. Two screens give the true state everywhere but at the serve, where both are the same screen. Since the cut is not an ending, these are the values of a rally that never ends. One step before a hit the value is 1 + 0.912 + 0.924 + ⋯ = 1/(1 − 0.912) ≈ 1.39, with a hit every 12 steps from then on; just after a hit the next one is 12 steps away, so the value is 0.911 × 1.39 ≈ 0.44, and it grows by a factor of 1/0.9 at every step until the next hit; at a serve it is 0.95 × 1.39 ≈ 0.82. When a miss is certain k steps ahead, the value is −0.9k−1: −1 one step before it, −0.9 two steps before, −0.81 three.

With a value for each sequence, or even for each pair of screens, the agent would learn nothing about a screen it had not seen. This game's pairs of screens are few enough for a table, 1,336 can occur, but an Atari game's are not, so the paper estimates Q* with weights θ, as the approximation notes estimated values with weights:

Q(s, a; θ) ≈ Q*(s, a)

Those notes' estimates were linear in features someone chose. A linear estimate with the pixels as its features fails at this game: trained as below, none of 16 agents ever hit the ball ten times from every serve, at any of four step sizes from 0.01 to 0.3. Each lit pixel adds its own weight, so the estimate cannot say that moving up is good when the ball is above the paddle and bad when it is below, wherever the two are; the control note built such combinations by hand, a feature for each situation and push. A network learns them from the pixels.

A network is built in layers, each multiplying what it receives by a table of weights and passing the result through a fixed function (the network of the word2vec note). Here the function is the rectifier, max(0, z), which keeps a positive number and turns a negative one into 0. The figures' network has two layers: 96 inputs, the pixels of the two screens; 32 rectifier units, its hidden layer; and 3 outputs, one value for each move:

Q(φ, ·; θ) = W2 max(0, W1φ)

with a 1 in front of φ and in front of the hidden layer, so that the first column of W1 and of W2 holds the biases, as in the word vectors note; θ is W1 (32 × 97) and W2 (3 × 33), 3,203 weights. The network reads φ(s), so the paper's Q(s, a; θ) is computed as Q(φ(s), a; θ).

One output per move is the paper's choice: earlier networks took the action as an input beside the history and needed a pass for each action, as the control note's q(s, a, w) needed a product for each push; this one gives every move's value in one pass. The paper's own network begins with two convolutional layers, which slide one small set of weights over the whole image so that a pattern is found wherever it appears (section 3's table gives its sizes); this game's screen is small enough for a layer that joins every pixel to every unit.

In the earlier notes' notation, θ is the w of the approximation notes, Q(s, a; θ) their q(s, a, w) and Q* the q* of the Q-learning note; rt is the reward that follows at, which they write Rt+1, so the return Rt is their Gt.

Where a move decides a hit, the network's best move is one that hits, and its value is near its red line; the values of moves the agent seldom makes in a situation stay rough, since only its random moves teach them. 2Learning from its own estimates

Q* obeys the Bellman optimality equation of the Q-learning note, here in the paper's notation (its equation 1):

Q*(s, a) = Es′∼ℰ[r + γ maxa′ Q*(s′, a′) | s, a]

Value iteration, which the model-based note ran on the values of states, turns it into an update repeated iteration after iteration, and converges to Q*:

Qi+1(s, a) = E[r + γ maxa′ Qi(s′, a′) | s, a]

It needs a value for every sequence, though, and learns nothing about one sequence from another.

The paper keeps value iteration's shape with a network. At iteration i it fits the weights θi to targets computed with the weights of the iteration before, θi−1, by making a loss small, a number that says how wrong the estimates are, as the VE of the prediction note did (the paper's equation 2):

Li(θi) = Es,a∼ρ(·)[(yi − Q(s, a; θi))2]

where the target is

yi = Es′∼ℰ[r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′; θi−1) | s, a]

ρ(s, a) is the paper's behavior distribution, how often each sequence and action come up as the agent plays. It does the job of the prediction note's μ, and it is not the importance-sampling ratio ρ of the n-step note. θi−1 is not a second network: it is the weights as they stand before a step, and θi the weights that the step changes.

So the targets depend on the weights. In supervised learning each example's target, its label, is fixed before learning begins; here every step that changes the weights can change the targets too.

The figure takes the game's first step from a serve: the paddle stays, the reward is 0, and the next pair of screens is φ2. It lets the network, with its random starting weights, learn from that one transition alone, one gradient step on Li per iteration, each step moving Q(φ1, stay; θ) toward its target as the prediction note's steps moved an estimate toward its target, and it draws two such runs side by side: the left one keeps the first target, as a label would be kept; the right one computes it again before every step, from the weights as they stand. The value is the blue line, its target the red one.

The target moved because one network computes both values. The step that raised staying in φ1 changed staying's own output weights, which every screen uses, and the hidden units fed by the pixels the two screens share, 5 of their 6 lit ones; so it raised staying in φ2 too, by about four fifths as much. Before the first step, moving down was φ2's largest value, just above staying (0.1858 against 0.1856); after it, staying is, so the target borrows the very value the steps raise. Times γ = 0.9, the target gains about 0.73 of what the value gains at each step, so the gap shrinks by about a fifth per step, until the two meet at 0.329, where the value equals its own target: a fixed point, as the TD fixed point of the prediction note was for a linear estimate. Had a step raised the next screen's value by more than 1/0.9 times as much as the value it updates, the target would have run ahead faster than the value could follow, and both would have grown without bound: learning from its own estimates can diverge, as section 4 takes up again. Learned alone in the same way, each from the starting weights, episode 1's other transitions settle well above their first target (0.152 → 0.562, 0.506 → 0.981), below it (0.104 → 0.053), or barely away from it (0.230 → 0.222, where the step raises moving up while the target borrows moving down); the last one, which ends the episode, keeps its target, the reward −1, which no step can move.

To make Li small, the paper follows its gradient. With θi−1 held fixed it is (the paper's equation 3, with a factor added):

−½∇θiLi(θi) = Es,a∼ρ(·); s′∼ℰ[(r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′; θi−1) − Q(s, a; θi)) ∇θiQ(s, a; θi)]

The paper prints its equation 3 without the −½: its right side is then the direction a descent step takes, not the gradient itself, which points the other way and is twice as long. The prediction note's ½ gave the same step, w ← w − ½α∇[…]2 = w + α[…]∇v (prediction note). ∇θQ still says which weights produced the value, and how much, as there; for a network it is computed layer by layer, from the output back, by the chain rule of the linear classifiers note, a computation called backpropagation.

Holding θi−1 fixed is the paper's way of writing what the prediction note called a semi-gradient: the step follows how the weights move the estimate, and ignores how they move the target.

Rather than computing the expectations, the paper takes stochastic gradient steps; with a single sample of ρ and of ℰ after every time step, the update is

θ ← θ + α[r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′; θ) − Q(s, a; θ)] ∇θQ(s, a; θ)

This is Q-learning. With a table, a weight for each sequence and move, ∇θQ is 1 for the pair updated and 0 for every other, and the step is the update of the Q-learning note, where the expectation became the one reward and next state just observed. With the value of the next move in place of the maximum, it would be the control note's semi-gradient Sarsa: only the maximum and the network are new.

An earlier method, neural fitted Q-learning (NFQ), minimizes the same losses with batch updates over all the data, at a cost per iteration that grows with the size of the data; deep Q-learning takes stochastic steps, at a small cost per iteration that stays the same however much data there is.

Like the methods of the TD note, it is model-free: it learns from samples of ℰ and builds no model of it. And it is off-policy, as in the Q-learning note: it learns about the greedy policy, a = argmaxa Q(s, a; θ), while it plays ε-greedy. The paper writes max where it means argmax, here and in its algorithm.

3Deep Q-learning

Deep Q-learning adds one thing to that update: a memory. After every step the agent stores the transition, et = (st, at, rt, st+1), in a data set,

𝒟 = e1, …, eN

the replay memory, which keeps the last N transitions, pooled over many episodes (this N is the memory's size, not the visit counts of the model-based note). Learning from such a memory, experience replay, goes back to Lin (1993). Each update draws a minibatch of transitions at random from 𝒟, 32 in the paper, and takes one gradient step on their average: between the prediction note's one state per step and the TD note's batch updating, which used them all at once. Dyna-Q, in the model-based note, also learned from remembered steps drawn at random, but its model kept only the last outcome of each pair, where 𝒟 keeps every recent transition. Section 4 says why the draws are random.

The algorithm

Deep Q-learning with experience replay, for a network Q with weights θ, a memory of N transitions, minibatches of 32, a step size α and an ε that falls over time, as the Monte Carlo note let it shrink, for M episodes:

  1. Start with 𝒟 empty, with room for N transitions, and θ at random.
  2. At the start of each episode, observe the first screen x1, and set s1 = x1 and φ1 = φ(s1).
  3. With probability ε choose a random action at; otherwise at = argmaxa Q(φt, a; θ).
  4. Take at, observe rt and xt+1, and set st+1 = st, at, xt+1 and φt+1 = φ(st+1).
  5. Store (φt, at, rt, φt+1) in 𝒟, dropping the oldest transition once it holds N.
  6. Draw a minibatch of transitions (φj, aj, rj, φj+1) at random from 𝒟, and set each one's target yj (below).
  7. Take one gradient step on their squared errors, with the targets held,
    θ ← θ + α · 132 Σj[yj − Q(φj, aj; θ)] ∇θQ(φj, aj; θ)
    If φt+1 is terminal, or the rally has reached the game's cut, start the next episode at step 2; otherwise go back to step 3.

The targets of step 6 are

yj = rj, if φj+1 is terminal;
yj = rj + γ maxa′ Q(φj+1, a′; θ), otherwise.

Here j counts the transitions drawn, where section 2's i counted iterations; with one step per iteration, the targets use θ as it stands before the step, the θi−1 of section 2, and α absorbs the 2 of the squared error, as there. The paper's algorithm writes Q* for the network's Q (and max for argmax, as section 2 said), and it calls the networks trained this way deep Q-networks, DQN. A rally cut after 10 hits is not terminal, so its last target still adds γ times the next screen's value.

The paper gives its reasons for two of the settings. Scores differ in scale from game to game, so in training the paper counts every positive reward as +1 and every negative one as −1, r ← sign(r): the errors, and so the steps, stay of similar size, and one step size suits every game; the price is that a large reward and a small one look the same, and the results use the games' real scores. And the agent chooses a move only on every 4th frame, repeating it on the frames between: emulating a frame costs far less than choosing a move, so it plays about four times as many games in the same time. Space Invaders is the one exception, every 3rd frame, since its lasers blink in a way that hides them at every 4th.

RMSProp is a form of gradient descent that divides each weight's step by the typical size of its recent gradients, so that every weight moves by a similar amount. The figures take plain gradient steps, as the algorithm above writes them: each moves a value part of the way to its target, which the readouts can show.

4Why experience replay

The agents of the earlier notes learned from each move as it was made, and so did TD-Gammon, an earlier program that learned backgammon with a network: each step was used once, in the order it came. Learning from a memory changes three things, which the paper lists as its advantages:

  1. Each step is used in many updates: transition 12 of section 3's figure was drawn 243 times while it stayed in the memory, as Dyna-Q's planning got more out of each real step.
  2. Consecutive steps are strongly correlated, the opening's second obstacle: one pair of screens differs from the next by a pixel or two. The prediction note warned that steps that wiped out each state's error would just chase the last state visited; learning from transitions in order chases the current rally in the same way. Draws from the last 10,000 steps break the correlation, and lower the variance of the updates.
  3. When an agent learns from the data its current weights produce, the weights choose the next data they learn from, the opening's third obstacle (the paper calls this learning on-policy, in a looser sense than the Q-learning note's). The behavior policy decides which pairs get visited, and so updated, as the Q-learning note said: a network that prefers moving up keeps its paddle near the top (a policy that moves up four times in five has it there on 88% of its screens), so it trains mostly on screens with the paddle at the top, and if it comes to prefer moving down, its data follow. Such loops can trap the weights or make them diverge. Replay averages the data over many past behaviors.

Replay makes learning off-policy, in section 2's sense, whether one wants it or not: a drawn transition holds a move that older weights chose, often at random (when section 3's figure reaches ten hits in a row, at step 8,094, 23 of the 32 transitions drawn held a move the network would not choose now). Sarsa's target uses the move that came next, which older weights chose too, so with replay it would learn about old policies; Q-learning's target takes the best move of the next screen under the weights as they stand, which is why the paper uses Q-learning. Its one-step target needs no correction for the other policy, as the Q-learning note showed: given the pair it updates, the reward and the next screen do not depend on who chose the move.

The paper also names the limits of its memory: it keeps only the last N transitions, dropping the oldest whatever they hold, and draws every transition alike, though in this run only 3 or 4 of each 32 draws carry a reward of +1 or −1; it suggests favoring the transitions there is most to learn from.

The figure runs section 3's agent 16 times, from 16 seeds, with three ways of choosing each step's minibatch. Replay draws 32 transitions at random from the memory. Last 32 takes the 32 most recent, in order: the same work per step, but on consecutive transitions, all made by nearly the current weights, so it loses the second and third advantages. Latest only learns from the newest transition alone, once, and loses the first as well. Every 250 steps a check, as in section 3's figure, plays each of the 12 serves with no random moves, and each curve counts the agents it finds perfect, ten hits from every serve; the runs go on to step 30,000, since without replay the agents need longer. Replay's curve is blue, the other two faint, Latest only's dashed.

Last 32's agents keep falling back, even after ε has reached 0.1 and nine moves in ten are their own: the kind of oscillation the paper says replay smooths out, though the figure shows the falls, not their cause. At a smaller step size, α = 0.03, agents without replay learn too, and replay is no faster (a median of 25,500 steps to the first perfect check, against 21,500 for Latest only, over 8 seeds): replay's gain here is that it stays stable at a step size large enough to learn fast.

With a step three times larger, α = 0.3, the difference is starker. With replay, all 16 agents play every serve perfectly, from a median of 4,000 steps, and their average largest value stays below 5.4. Without it, none of the 32 agents ever does, and on 17 of them the values grow past 1020, or past the largest number the computer can hold, all of them within the first 1,400 steps, while at least 87% of the moves are still random. So it is not the loop between the weights and the data that drives it here: half of the Last 32 agents blow up just the same when every move is random. What differs between the three ways is only which transitions each step learns from. Without replay, each step learns from the last few transitions, nearly the same screens, and pushes the same weights again and again, toward targets those weights set; drawn from 10,000 steps, the pushes average out.

The paper cites results showing that Q-learning can diverge when its estimate is not linear, such as a network (Tsitsiklis and Van Roy, 1997), or when it learns off-policy (Baird, 1995). It offers no guarantee that its own method converges, and it reports that none of its runs diverged.

5Measuring progress

How can one tell that the agent is learning? The paper's measure of play is the total reward per episode, averaged over the episodes of a stretch of ε-greedy play with ε = 0.05. It is noisy, because a small change to the weights can change which states the policy visits. A steadier measure looks at the values instead: before training, collect a fixed set of states by playing at random, and follow the average, over them, of the largest value the network predicts,

1500 Σφ maxa Q(φ, a; θ)

the sum running over the 500 pairs of screens φ collected that way.

The figure follows section 3's run with both measures, every 250 steps: on top, the total reward per episode over 10,000 steps of play with ε = 0.05, as the paper measured it; below, the average largest value over 500 pairs of screens collected by random play before training. A dashed line marks step 8,000, the last check at which greedy play missed a serve.

In this game the average is not the steady rise of the paper's runs. It jumps from 0.17 to 0.93 in the first 500 steps and falls back to 0.34 by step 1,500. From there it climbs to about 1.0 by step 7,750, while the total reward stays below 2 until step 6,500: progress that the values show and play does not yet. While the agent learns to play, from step 6,750 to 8,250, the total reward jumps up and down as greedy play wins or loses whole groups of serves, and the average moves a few percent: from 7,000 to 7,250 greedy play goes from 5 perfect serves to none and the total reward from 4.21 to 1.17, while the average moves from 0.97 to 0.94. Once every ball is hit, the ball's path settles into one of two cycles, six serves on each, so a small change that breaks one costs six serves at once. From step 8,250 on, both measures are steady: the average stays between 1.03 and 1.31 until step 14,000, then drifts down to 0.93 at step 20,000, still above the exact average over the same screens, 0.79: the learned values end too high, as section 6 shows screen by screen.

6What the values say

The paper also shows what the values say during play: over 30 frames of Seaquest, the predicted value jumps when an enemy appears, peaks as a torpedo is about to hit it, and falls once the enemy is gone. In this game no reward ever appears suddenly, so there is no jump, but the peak and the fall are there, and section 1's exact values give the shape to compare: a rise toward each hit, a fall right after it, and −1 one step before a certain miss. The network's values follow that shape but run high: one step before a hit they reach 1.5 to 1.65, where the exact value is 1.39. Every target takes a maximum over estimates, which, as the maximization bias of the Q-learning note showed, tends to come out high; it may contribute here.

The key ideas next to their NumPy equivalents, for the figures' network and settings. x_prev and x are the last two screens, 48 numbers each; w1 and w2 are the network's weights, each with a first column of biases, and theta holds them as one vector; q holds the three values of a pair of screens. rs holds the rewards of an episode from t on, and g is the return Rt. In the minibatch rows, r, done and q_a hold the 32 rewards, whether each next pair of screens is terminal (1 or 0), and the values of the moves taken; q_next holds the values of each next pair of screens, as rows, and grad_q the gradient of each Q(φj, aj; θ) with respect to the weights, as rows, which backpropagation computes. q_fixed holds the values of the 500 fixed pairs of screens. The step is the figures' plain gradient step, α absorbing the 2 of the squared error as section 2 explained; the paper's optimizer is RMSProp.
