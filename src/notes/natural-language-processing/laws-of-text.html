Search engines, sentiment analysis, information extraction, machine translation and question answering are different problems with the same raw material: text, whose only structure is its words. Before building any of them, it pays to look at how words are used. Count every word of a long book, here Don Quixote in John Ormsby's 1885 translation, from its first chapter to its last line, and three facts stand out:

  • Ten words make up a quarter of the book

  • A third of its different words appear only once

  • Its vocabulary still grows on the last page

The first two are about how often each word is used, which Zipf's law describes; the third is about how many different words a text uses, which Heaps' law describes. Both shape what comes later: which words a system can set aside, how large its vocabulary must be, and why it will always meet words it has never seen.

On this page, counts from the book are drawn in red ink and the laws fitted to them in blue.

1A few words do most of the work

Sort the book's words from the most frequent to the least. A word's place in that list is its rank, r, and the number of times it appears is its frequency, fr. Each appearance is a token: the book has 405,684 tokens of 14,927 different words. “The”, rank 1, accounts for 20,747 of them, “and”, rank 2, for 16,917, and “like”, rank 100, for 554. In 1949 George Kingsley Zipf observed that in long texts the frequency falls with the rank in the same way, as a power of it:

fr = Crα

The constant C sets the scale and the exponent α how fast the frequencies fall; in long texts α is usually close to 1. With α = 1, rank times frequency is the same for every word: a word with ten times the rank appears a tenth as often. Zipf explained it with what he called the principle of least effort. Speaking costs least when the same handy words serve again and again, instead of the exact word for each thing, so a few words end up everywhere and the many words for particular things each turn up rarely.

2A straight line on log scales

On the figure's axes the words fell close to a straight line, and the law says why. Take logarithms of both sides, in base 10 like the axes:

log fr = log C − α log r

With xr = log r and yr = log fr, this is a line: its slope is −α, and its height at r = 1, where xr = 0, is log C. Estimating α and C means finding the line that follows the points best. Least squares takes the line that makes the vertical distances from the points to it, squared and added up, as small as possible, and for a line the answer has a closed form:

α = −Σr (xr − x)(yr − y)Σr (xr − x)2, log C = y + αx

Here x and y are the means of the xr and of the yr. Squaring makes a large miss count more than two small ones, and treats misses above and below the line alike.

For this book least squares gives α = 1.11 and C = 82,377. Through the middle of the list the line is close: at rank 1,000 it gives 38, where “settled” appears 37 times. But it passes far above the first word, 82,377 against the 20,747 of “the”, the unevenness section 1 found at the top of the list.

C and the length of the book

If the law has to account for every token of the book, C is not free: the frequencies of the V different words must add up to n, the number of tokens. That fixes it:

fr = nrα HV(α), HV(α) = ΣVj=1 1jα

With α = 1 the sum is close to ln V + γ, where γ ≈ 0.577 is a constant. For this book ln 14,927 + 0.577 ≈ 10.19, so C = 405,684 / 10.19 ≈ 39,800: the 40,000 that rank times frequency kept returning to in section 1.

3The vocabulary keeps growing

Now read the book from the start and count the different words met so far. After n tokens the vocabulary has V words, and V grows for as long as there is text: names, rare words and new forms of known words keep turning up. But it grows ever more slowly, as a power of n below 1. This is Heaps' law:

V = k nβ

The constant k and the exponent β depend on the text and its language; β is usually between 0.4 and 0.6. On log scales this law is a line too, log V = log k + β log n, so least squares finds β and k as it found α and C. What β means shows when the text doubles:

V(2n)V(n) = k(2n)βknβ = 2β

Twice the text brings 2β times the words, not twice as many. The figure shows the book's vocabulary as it is read, with Heaps' law fitted to it in blue and, faint, the Spanish original's. Drag the red dot through the book; the ring marks twice as far.

For this translation least squares gives β = 0.52 and k = 18.5, so doubling the text multiplies the vocabulary by about 20.52 ≈ 1.43; the counted ratios, from 10,000 tokens on, run from 1.34 to 1.57. The two numbers are tied: a slightly smaller β with a larger k draws nearly the same curve over the book, and with β fixed at 0.5 the best k is 23.7. The Spanish original uses 22,606 different words in fewer tokens, with β = 0.62, because Spanish inflects: a verb takes dozens of forms, and each counts as a different word. The next note is about those forms.

4The tail never runs out

Zipf's law and Heaps' law look at the same thing from two sides. Zipf's law sorts the words by frequency and finds a long tail of rare ones; Heaps' law follows the text and finds new words arriving all along. The new words come from that tail: each stretch of text draws a few words from a list too long to empty. Split the vocabulary met so far by how often each word has been seen, and the words seen once stay a large part of it however far you read.

After the first 1,000 tokens, 77% of the words met have been seen once; after 100,000, 46%; at the end, 37%. The share falls, but slowly, and new words keep coming: 314 in the 10,000 tokens before token 100,000, and still 155 in the book's last 10,000. A system that learns its vocabulary from one text will meet words it has never seen in the next, a problem later notes return to.
