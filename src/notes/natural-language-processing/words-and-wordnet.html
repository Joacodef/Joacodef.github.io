The previous note ended on a gap: the Spanish original uses 22,605 different words where the translation uses 14,921, because a Spanish verb takes dozens of forms and a count takes each for a different word. To a reader, said, says and saying are one verb; to a count, three words. The opposite also happens, one spelling standing for several words. This note looks at what words are made of and what they mean, with the tools NLP uses for both:

  • Three words to a count, one verb to a reader

  • The same words: a place, or part of a name

  • One spelling, two words: brightness and not heavy

Sections 1 and 2 are about forms: what a word is made of, and two ways of gathering a word's forms, which bring the two books' vocabularies much closer. Section 3 labels each word of a sentence with its part of speech and finds the names in it. Sections 4 and 5 are about meanings: how WordNet relates them, and how one spelling can carry several.

1What a word is made of

Linguistics describes language at four levels, each with the unit it studies and the discipline that studies it:

LevelUnitDiscipline
Morphologicalthe wordmorphology
Syntacticthe sentencesyntax
Lexical-semanticthe meaninglexicology and semantics
Textualthe texttext analytics

An NLP system may need any of them; this note covers the first three, starting with the word. Its smallest piece with both a form and a meaning is the moneme, and there are two kinds. Speaking holds one of each: speak, the lexeme, carries the meaning and is shared with related words such as speaker; -ing, a morpheme, adds grammatical meaning, here an action in progress. Morphemes mark gender, number, person, tense and the like.

Words split into two groups by whether they take such morphemes. Variable words, such as nouns, adjectives, verbs and pronouns, change their form; invariable ones, such as prepositions, conjunctions and interjections, keep a single form. Spanish puts person, number, tense and mood into its verbs' endings, where English uses separate words: diremos is we will say. A count takes the Spanish form for one more word, and the English words for three it already has; section 2 measures how much of the gap between the books that explains.

2Stems and lemmas

Two methods gather a word's forms. Stemming cuts endings off by rules, with no dictionary: the Snowball stemmers, which grew out of Martin Porter's 1980 stemmer for English and now cover many languages, turn speaking and speaks into speak. What remains, the stem, need not be a word. Lemmatization returns each form's lemma, the form a dictionary lists it under: said and says both give say. A lemmatizer never invents a word, but it needs each form's part of speech, since saw is a form of see as a verb and a word of its own as a noun; without it, NLTK's lemmatizer takes every word for a noun.

Each method fails in its own way, as the examples show, but lemmatizing brings the books close: the translation's 14,921 different words become 10,337 lemmas, and the original's 22,605 become 12,596. A word the list lacks counts as its own lemma. That is right for most of them, since the list leaves out the words that are lemmas already, such as with, but the Spanish list also lacks verbs with a pronoun attached, such as decirle (to tell him), and the old spellings of a book from 1605, such as agora for ahora (now), so the original's true count is lower still.

3Parts of speech and names

Sentences have parts too. Their words form groups, the constituents, and smaller groups sit inside larger ones. In The squire rides a donkey, the squire is a noun phrase (NP): he can take its place, as in He rides a donkey, which shows that the two words work as one. Rides a donkey is a verb phrase (VP), a verb with a noun phrase of its own.

Parsers recover this structure, often starting from each word's category, its part of speech: noun, verb, preposition and so on. Labeling every word with it is part-of-speech tagging (POS tagging). Named-entity recognition (NER) reads the same words with another question: not what kind of word each one is, but what it refers to, such as a person, a place, an organization or a date.

Mancha is a proper noun both times, so its part of speech cannot tell a place from part of a name. The clues come from two sources. The form of a word, such as a capital letter, says it may be a name; its neighbors say what kind: after a village of comes a place, after Don a person. NER is called a morphosyntactic task for that reason: it needs both the word's form and its place in the sentence.

4Meanings in a network

Meanings are related too, and two relations matter most. A knight-errant is a kind of knight: knight-errant is a hyponym of knight, and knight a hypernym of knight-errant. A helmet is a piece of a suit of armor: helmet is a meronym of suit of armor, and suit of armor a holonym of helmet. The first relation sorts things into classes; the second takes them apart.

WordNet records both for English, and it is organized by meanings rather than by words: each synset is a set of synonyms with one meaning, a definition, and links to other synsets. George Miller's team at Princeton began building it by hand in 1985; version 3.0 has 117,659 synsets and 147,306 different words and phrases. NLTK includes it, and its lemmatizer is built on it: an irregular form such as saw is looked up in a list of exceptions, and a regular one loses its ending only if what remains is in WordNet. BabelNet goes further: it merges WordNet with Wikipedia and other resources, so each of its entries gathers a concept's words in many languages, along with names of people and places that WordNet lacks.

5One spelling, several words

Synonymy is several forms for one meaning. The reverse, one form for several meanings, comes about in two ways. In polysemy, one word's senses grew apart from a shared origin, often by metaphor. In homonymy, two unrelated words share a spelling by chance. Dictionaries mark the difference: they give each homonym an entry of its own, and list the senses of a polysemous word inside its one entry. WordNet makes no such difference: it gives light 47 senses, 15 as a noun, 6 as a verb, 25 as an adjective and 1 as an adverb, with no mark of which ones share an origin.

The book's light shows both. Light as brightness comes from a root *leuk-, and light as not heavy from a different one, *legwh-: two words that look and sound alike by an accident of history. Each grew senses of its own. From brightness came a lamp, and the verb in to light a candle; from not heavy came to light upon something, which first meant to get down from a horse and so lighten its load, and to make light of it. Choosing a word's sense from its sentence is word-sense disambiguation, and a lemmatizer does not do it: light gets one lemma whatever it means. The word vectors of later notes share that blind spot, one vector per word, until encoders build each word's vector from its sentence.

6Summary
