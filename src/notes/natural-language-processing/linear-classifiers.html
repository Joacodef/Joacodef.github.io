Much of Don Quixote is the knight and his squire talking. Cover the names and a reader can often still tell who is speaking: one calls the other your worship, the other answers with thou and friend Sancho. A linear model can learn to do the same from the words alone. It gives each word a weight, adds up the weights of a speech's words and reads the sign of the sum. Trained on the speeches that the book attributes to one of the two, it names the speaker of 83% of the speeches from chapters it never saw:

  • Which of the two said it?

  • Each word pushes toward one of them

  • The pushes add up to a probability

Section 1 adds up the weights of a speech's words, section 2 draws the line that the sign of the sum makes between the two speakers, section 3 turns the sum into a probability, section 4 measures how wrong a probability is, section 5 learns the weights from the speeches, and section 6 adds the narrator as a third voice.

1A weight per word

A speech is a bag of words: a vector x that counts each word of the vocabulary in it, as each chapter was a vector of counts in the vector space model note. A linear model gives each word i a weight wi and adds up the counts times their weights into a score:

s = wTx = ∑Vi=0 wi xi.

The sum starts at i = 0, with x0 = 1 in every speech, so that w0, the bias, is the score of a speech with none of the vocabulary's words. Here a positive score means Sancho and a negative one Don Quixote. A word pushes toward whoever says it, not toward whoever it names: sancho pushes toward Don Quixote, who is the one that calls his squire by name. The weights in the figure were learned from the speeches of four chapters in every five (section 5 shows how), and its six speeches come from the fifth, which the model never saw. All 2,541 words of its vocabulary are kept, stopwords included: the don't and I'll that a search engine would drop are part of Sancho's voice.

The heaviest weights belong to the words with which each addresses the other: worship and master point to Sancho; thou, friend and sancho to Don Quixote. Words the model never met in training, such as cabbages, weigh 0. The last speech is Sancho's, but it addresses no one, and the small weights of its common words lean the other way: what this model reads, above all, is to whom a speech is spoken.

2A line between the two

A speech is a point in a space with a dimension for each word, and the sign of its score splits that space in two: Sancho's side, where s > 0, and Don Quixote's, where s < 0. The boundary between them, where s = 0, is a hyperplane perpendicular to w, and w points toward the positive side. With two words the space is a plane and the boundary a line, which can be drawn.

Take two of the heaviest words: worship, from the your worship with which Sancho addresses his master, and thou, with which his master addresses him. Each speech sits at its counts of the two, and many share a point: 817 of the 1,232 contain neither word and pile up at (0, 0). With only these two words the score is s = w0 + w1x1 + w2x2. 3From a score to a probability

A score says on which side of the line a speech falls, and how far from it, but not how sure to be. Three classic models compute the same score and differ only in the function θ they apply to it:

y = θ(s) = θ(wTx).

The perceptron keeps only the sign, a step from 0 for Don Quixote to 1 for Sancho: it decides, but says nothing of its confidence, and a jump has no slope to learn from. Linear regression keeps the score itself, any real number, which suits a quantity to predict, not a class. Logistic regression squeezes the score between 0 and 1 with the logistic function, smooth enough to learn from its slope, and reads the result as a probability:

h(x) = θ(wTx) = 11 + e−wTx = P[y = 1 | x],

the probability that Sancho, y = 1, said the speech x, rather than Don Quixote, y = 0. Don Quixote's needs no second model: θ(−s) = 1 − θ(s), the same score with its sign turned. In the figure, drag the score and follow the three outputs.

Drag the red point along the score, or move it with the arrow keys.

Far from 0 the logistic flattens: a score of 4 or of 8 gives nearly the same certainty, near 1, as the step would. Near 0 it rises steeply, and there a small change in the score moves the probability most.

4How wrong a probability is

To learn its weights, the model needs one number that says how wrong its probabilities are on the speeches whose speaker it knows. For one speech, look at the probability p it gave the right speaker: h(x) if Sancho spoke, 1 − h(x) if Don Quixote did. A good model makes p large for all the speeches at once, that is, makes their product large; the logarithm turns that product into a sum, and a minus sign turns it into a loss to make small. Averaged over the N speeches, with natural logarithms, this is the binary cross-entropy:

ℒ(w) = −1N ∑Nn=1 [yn log h(xn) + (1 − yn) log(1 − h(xn))].

Since each yn is 1 or 0, one of the two terms vanishes for every speech, and what is left is −log p. A right answer given with confidence, p near 1, costs almost nothing; a wrong one given with confidence, p near 0, costs without bound. In the figure, drag the probability h that the model gives Sancho, and choose who really spoke.

The two curves are mirror images: Sancho's loss is small where the model leans to him, Don Quixote's where it leans away. That is the asymmetry the loss is built for: hesitating costs a little, and being confidently wrong costs a lot.

5Learning the weights

Learning means finding the w that makes ℒ smallest. The loss depends on w through the probability h, which depends on the score s, which depends on w; by the chain rule, its derivative for one speech is the product of three links,

∂ℒn∂h = h − yh(1 − h), ∂h∂s = h(1 − h), ∂s∂w = x,

and the product is simply (h − y)x: the speech's error times the speech. Averaged over the speeches, it gives the gradient, which points to where the loss grows fastest, and gradient descent steps the other way, by a length set by the learning rate η:

∇ℒ(w) = 1N ∑Nn=1 (h(xn) − yn) xn, w ← w − η∇ℒ(w).

A speech that the model gives too much of Sancho pushes the weights of its words toward Don Quixote, and the reverse: the perceptron's rule, with a graded error instead of a yes or a no. With η = 0.5 the line soon finds its place, but the weights, and with them the probabilities, grow slowly: after thirty steps the loss, 0.569, is still far above its lowest, 0.538. With η = 5 the loss comes within 0.01 of its lowest in seven steps. With η = 16 the steps jump past the minimum, back and forth: the gradient changes sign at nearly every step, the weights swing and grow, and the loss climbs above where it started. The model of section 1 learned its 2,542 weights the same way, every word a dimension of the same descent.

6Three voices: softmax

The book has a third voice, the narrator's, who tells what the two do between their words. With K classes, w becomes a matrix with a row of weights per class, and each line gets a score from each row:

sk = wkTx, pk = esk∑j=1K esj.

The softmax turns the K scores into probabilities, all positive and adding up to 1, each the larger the further its score stands above the others; with K = 2 it is the logistic function again, applied to the difference of the two scores. Trained on the same chapters, with sentences of the narrator as a third class, the model names the voice of 76% of the lines from chapters it never saw. 7Summary

The note's ideas in NumPy. x holds the speeches' counts, a row per speech with a first column of ones, y their labels, 1 for Sancho and 0 for Don Quixote, and w the weights, the bias first; for three voices, w has a row per class.

s = x @ w
Logistic
h = 1 / (1 + e−s)
h = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-s))
Binary cross-entropy
ℒ = −(1/N) ∑ [y log h + (1 − y) log(1 − h)]
loss = -np.mean(
    y * np.log(h)
    + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - h))
Gradient
∇ℒ = (1/N) ∑ (h − y) x
grad = x.T @ (h - y) / len(y)
Gradient descent
w ← w − η∇ℒ
w -= eta * grad
