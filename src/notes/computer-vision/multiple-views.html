M = (QTQ)−1QTr

The point of space that best fits its pixels in two or more images.

m3 = ℓ13 × ℓ23

Where a point seen in two images appears in a third. The trifocal tensor finds it too, even where the two lines coincide.

Throughout this page, points are drawn in red ink and lines in blue ink. It uses the intersection of two lines from the note on homogeneous coordinates, least squares and the optical center from the note on camera calibration, and the fundamental matrix and epipolar lines from the note on epipolar geometry.

1Rays that should meet

One image fixes the ray on which a point of space lies, but not where on that ray. The note on epipolar geometry used a second image to find where the point can appear in it. This note goes two steps further. Given the pixels of a point in two or more images, it finds the point itself. And given its pixels in two images, it predicts where the point appears in a third.

A point M seen in image i as the pixel mi lies on the ray from the optical center Ci through mi. It lies on the ray of every image that sees it, so it is where those rays meet. Finding a point from the rays of its images is called triangulation, and finding it this way is 3D reconstruction. Here that word means locating a point. It is not what computed tomography reconstructs, which is what an object is made of at each of its points.

Real pixels are measured, and the cameras are estimated, so the rays almost never meet exactly, not even two of them. The question becomes which point of space best explains the pixels.

The example

The example on this page uses the X-ray images of the note on epipolar geometry: an object turns 2° about the vertical axis Z between one image and the next, and the X-ray source, the optical center, moves around a horizontal circle. In images 1, 40 and 90 the object has turned 2°, 80° and 180°. So the sources of images 1 and 40 are 78° apart around the circle, those of images 40 and 90 are 100° apart, and those of images 1 and 90 are 178° apart, almost facing each other. The two ends of a small object were clicked by hand in each of the three images. Lengths are in millimeters and image coordinates in pixels.

2Two equations per view

For one image, write its camera P with entries pjk, the pixel as m = [x y 1]T and the point as M = [X Y Z 1]T. The projection λm = PM is three equations:

λx = p11X + p12Y + p13Z + p14
λy = p21X + p22Y + p23Z + p24
λ = p31X + p32Y + p33Z + p34

The unknowns are X, Y and Z, and λ too, a new one for each image. The third equation gives λ outright: it is the depth of M in front of the camera, in millimeters in this example, since the first three entries of the third row of P form a unit vector. Putting it into the first two removes it:

(p31x − p11)X + (p32x − p12)Y + (p33x − p13)Z = p14 − p34x
(p31y − p21)X + (p32y − p22)Y + (p33y − p23)Z = p24 − p34y

That is why each image gives two equations: it brings three equations and one new unknown, λ, and one equation is spent on it. The two left are linear in X, Y and Z, because the pixel (x, y) is a known number.

Each of them has a picture. The first holds for every point whose image has this x, whatever its y: the points that project onto one column of the image. They form a plane through the optical center. The second is the plane through the center that projects onto the row y. The ray of m is where the two planes meet. So one image gives a line, not a point, and a second image is needed to fix a point on it.

3Least squares from n views

Each of n images gives its two rows, with its own camera and pixel. Stacked, they form one system Q[X Y Z]T = r, where Q is 2n × 3 and r has 2n entries, the two rows of image i in places 2i − 1 and 2i. As in the note on camera calibration, least squares gives the point that makes the sum of the squared entries of Q[X Y Z]T − r smallest:

[X Y Z]T = (QTQ)−1QTr

The hat marks an estimate. Call that point M. It exists when Q has rank 3, that is, when the 2n planes do not all share one line.

Two views and the epipolar constraint

Two images give four equations for three unknowns, one more than needed, so the four have a common solution only for some pairs of pixels. Write them as [Q | −r][X Y Z 1]T = 0: a 4 × 4 system with right side 0, which has a solution other than 0 only when det [Q | −r] = 0. Each row of [Q | −r] is xp3 − p1 or yp3 − p2 for one of the two pixels, with pj the rows of its camera. So the determinant is linear in m1 and linear in m2, which is the form of m2TFm1, and it vanishes for every pair of corresponding points. It is the epipolar constraint, times a factor that depends only on the cameras. For images 1 and 40, with F = [BC1]×BA+ as in the note on epipolar geometry, the factor is −6.33·107, whatever the pixels. It is the determinant det G of that note, with λ1 and λ2 already eliminated.

So two rays meet exactly when their pixels satisfy the epipolar constraint. The clicks of the example do not. In image 40, they lie 23.75 and 13.23 pixels from the epipolar lines of their partners in image 1, as the note on epipolar geometry found, so their rays miss each other. Least squares splits the difference: with images 1 and 40 only, the upper end's M projects 10.90 pixels from its click in image 1 and 11.87 from the one in image 40.

Views that face each other

The rays of two images must cross at a clear angle. The sources of images 1 and 90 are 178° apart, on opposite sides of the object, so the two rays through a point of the object are almost one line: they meet at about 174°. Along that line the two images barely see a difference, and a few pixels of error move M far along it. With only these two images the object comes out 67.43 mm long, while images 1 and 40 give 45.23 mm and images 40 and 90 give 45.32 mm. In the equations, the columns of Q are nearly dependent: its singular values are 11258, 11245 and 596.5, against 11269, 8832 and 6977 for images 1 and 40.

More views

With three images, Q is 6 × 3. The errors of the clicks partly cancel, and no single pair of images decides the result. Images 1, 40 and 90 together give 45.46 mm.

4What the fit minimizes

Least squares makes QM − r small, writing M for [X Y Z]T, but what is that residual? Take the first row of image i. Its left side minus its right side is (p3 · M)xi − p1 · M, writing pj · M for the product of row j of Pi with [X Y Z 1]T. The first product is the depth λi of M, and the second is λixi, where mi = (xi, yi) is where M projects into image i. So each row of the residual is λi(xi − xi) or λi(yi − yi), a depth in millimeters times a pixel error, and least squares minimizes

‖QM − r‖2 = Σi λi2 [(xi − xi)2 + (yi − yi)2]

That is the error in pixels weighted by the squared depth, in mm·px: an image whose source is farther from the point weighs more. The quantity with a plain meaning is the reprojection error, the distance in pixels from each clicked mi to the projection mi of the point found, with λimi = PiM. The best point in that sense minimizes

J(M) = Σi (xi − xi)2 + (yi − yi)2

No formula gives that point, because xi and yi divide by the depth, which depends on M. It is found by iterating, with a gradient method for example, starting from M. When all the depths are alike, the two answers nearly agree. Here they run from 908 to 1019 mm: minimizing J moves the ends by 0.18 and 0.20 mm, and changes the length by 0.005 mm.

Pick the images that take part. In the close-ups, the dots are the clicks and the rings are where the point found projects; in an image left out, the hollow dots are its clicks and the rings are a prediction. In the lower view, the rays come from the X-ray sources through the clicked pixels, and the two red points are the ends found. Zoom in to see the rays miss each other by a millimeter or two: the dashed segments join each end to each of its rays. Drag the background of the lower view to turn it. With a keyboard, select a button or the lower view with Tab and use the arrow keys.

5A third view from two

Now three images, with cameras A, B and C. Given corresponding points m1 and m2 in the first two, where does their point of space appear in the third? Epipolar geometry already answers it. m3 lies on the epipolar line of m1 in image 3, and on that of m2:

ℓ13 = F13m1,  ℓ23 = F23m2

where F13 is the fundamental matrix of images 1 and 3, from A and C, and F23 that of images 2 and 3, from B and C. So m3 is where the two lines cross, and the point where two lines cross is their cross product, as in the note on homogeneous coordinates:

m3 = ℓ13 × ℓ23

It is also a test. m1, m2 and m3 correspond exactly when m3 is that point: the condition is necessary and sufficient.

When the two lines are one

The crossing is not defined when ℓ13 and ℓ23 are the same line. ℓ13 is where image 3 cuts the epipolar plane of M, C1 and C3, and ℓ23 is where it cuts the plane of M, C2 and C3. Both planes contain M and C3, and they are the same plane when it holds C1 and C2 as well. That happens in two ways:

  • The three optical centers are on one line, as when one camera takes the three images while moving in a straight line. This is common, and then it happens for every point.
  • The centers are not on one line, but M lies on the plane through the three of them.

Near either case the lines cross at a shallow angle, and a small error in m1 or m2 moves their crossing far. And even when the lines cross well, the method takes two steps, two lines and then their crossing, rather than one formula.

In the example

Every source of the X-ray series is on one circle, so the plane through any three of them is the plane of the circle, Z = −21.94. It passes through every optical center, so each camera sees it edge-on, as a line, and it is the same line in every image: from v = 1284.57 at u = 0 to v = 1285.63 at u = 2688, practically the row v ≈ 1285.

The lower end of the object lies on that plane: the point found in section 3 is 0.19 mm above it. Predicted in image 90 from its clicks in images 1 and 40, the two epipolar lines cross at only 6.33°, and their crossing, (1503.11, 1283.15), lands 100.99 pixels from where the end was clicked in image 90, (1603, 1298). Right on that row, one pixel of error in m2 moves the crossing by a thousand pixels or more. The upper end is 45 mm above the plane. There the lines cross at 65.35°, and their crossing lands 8.18 pixels from its click.

6The trifocal tensor

A way around the epipolar lines starts from the three projections themselves. Pick a 4 × 4 matrix H whose first three rows are A, and change coordinates to M̃ = HM. Since A = [I | 0]H,

Ã = AH−1 = [I | 0],  B̃ = BH−1,  C̃ = CH−1

and λ1m1 = ÃM̃, λ2m2 = B̃M̃, λ3m3 = C̃M̃: the same projections, in coordinates where the first camera is as simple as it can be. H must be invertible. Adding the row [0 0 0 1] under A works whenever C1 is a finite point, because then the left 3 × 3 block of A is invertible.

The ray of m1

λ1m1 = [I | 0]M̃ says that the first three components of M̃ are λ1m1. With the fourth equal to 1,

M̃ = [λ1x1 λ1y1 λ1 1]T = λ1[x1 y1 1 1/λ1]T

As λ1 runs over all depths, M̃ runs along the ray of m1. Finding the point means finding λ1.

The depth from one coordinate

Project this M̃ into image 2 with B̃. Write b̃j for the first three entries of row j of B̃, as a row, and b̃j4 for its last one. The first coordinate of the pixel is x2 = (λ1b̃1m1 + b̃14) / (λ1b̃3m1 + b̃34), and solved for λ1:

λ1 = b̃14 − b̃34x2x2b̃3m1 − b̃1m1

This is the depth at which the ray of m1 projects onto the column x2 of image 2: where it crosses the plane through C2 that images onto that column, as in section 2. The same step in image 3, with the rows c̃k of C̃ and the column x3, gives λ1 = (c̃14 − c̃34x3) / (x3c̃3m1 − c̃1m1).

Four trilinearities

If m1, m2 and m3 correspond, the two depths are the same λ1. Setting them equal, multiplying out and collecting the terms in 1, x2, x3 and x2x3 gives an equation that is linear in m1, in x2 and in x3:

m1T(x3T13 − x3x2T33 + x2T31 − T11) = 0

Each Tjk here is a column of three numbers, Tjk = [T1jk T2jk T3jk]T, with

Tijk = b̃jic̃k4 − b̃j4c̃ki

for i, j, k = 1, 2, 3. Using y2 instead of x2, or y3 instead of x3, gives three more equations of the same kind. The four are the trilinearities:

m1T(x3T13 − x3x2T33 + x2T31 − T11) = 0
m1T(y3T13 − y3x2T33 + x2T32 − T12) = 0
m1T(x3T23 − x3y2T33 + y2T31 − T21) = 0
m1T(y3T23 − y3y2T33 + y2T32 − T22) = 0

The 27 numbers Tijk form the trifocal tensor of the three images. Like F, they depend only on the cameras, not on the points, so they are computed once for all the points. And like F, they are homogeneous: another fourth row for H multiplies all 27 by one number, which changes none of the four equations. The four equations are independent, and they hold only for corresponding points: when all four hold, m1, m2 and m3 correspond.

The same equations from a determinant

As with F in the note on epipolar geometry, the same equations come from the projections written as one system, Gv = 0. Here G is 9 × 7, with the rows of Ã, B̃ and C̃ in its first four columns and m1, m2 and m3 in its last three, and v = [M̃; −λ1; −λ2; −λ3]. Corresponding points give a solution v ≠ 0, so every 7 × 7 matrix made of 7 of the 9 rows of G has determinant 0. Leaving out two rows of one camera keeps only the other two cameras, and gives their epipolar constraint, such as m3TF23m2 = 0. Leaving out one row from each of two cameras keeps all three, and those determinants give the trilinearities.

7Transfer with the tensor

Knowing m1 and m2, the first two trilinearities are linear in x3 and in y3. Solved for them, they give the point in image 3 directly:

m3 = 1m1T(T13 − x2T33) m1T(T11 − x2T31)m1T(T12 − x2T32)m1T(T13 − x2T33)

Why it works: x2 fixes λ1, which is where the ray of m1 crosses the plane through C2 and the column x2, and that point of space, projected by C, is m3. It is a reconstruction from one image and a half, followed by a projection into the third. No epipolar lines are crossed, so the plane of the three centers does no harm. For the object's lower end, the transfer gives (1626.72, 1269.52), 37.06 pixels from the click in image 90, where the crossing of the epipolar lines was 100.99 pixels off. What is left is the clicks' own error: in images 1 and 40, they are 13.23 pixels from each other's epipolar line.

Its own weak spot

If the epipolar line of m1 in image 2 is the column x2 itself, the plane through C2 and that column holds the whole ray of m1, and x2 cannot fix λ1. The last two trilinearities, which use y2, then do the job. In the example, the epipolar lines are close to rows, since the epipoles lie on the row v ≈ 1285, mostly far to the sides of the images, so the form with x2 is the one to use. The form with y2 fails on that row, just as the crossing of epipolar lines does. For images whose sources face each other, such as 1 and 90, the epipoles fall inside the images, at (1207.46, 1285.05) in image 1. Then the form with x2 fails for pixels m1 close to a nearly vertical line through that epipole.

Is it a third match?

m1, m2 and m3 correspond if m1 and m2 satisfy the epipolar constraint, and the three satisfy the first two trilinearities, or the last two. The first two use x2 but never y2, and the epipolar constraint between m1 and m2 is what checks y2. With measured points, both tests get a tolerance:

|m2TF12m1|√(a2 + b2) < d0,  ‖m3 − m3‖ < d1

with F12m1 = [a b c]T, as in the note on epipolar geometry. The epipolar test comes first because the transfer is only as good as the pair it starts from. The upper end's clicks in images 1 and 40 are 23.75 pixels off each other's epipolar line, and its transfer lands 34.95 pixels from its click in image 90, farther than the crossing of the epipolar lines, which lands 8.18 pixels from it.

8Four views and more

With a fourth image, the same tests chain. m1, m2, m3 and m4 correspond if m1, m2, m3 correspond and m2, m3, m4 correspond: the shared m2 and m3 fix one point of space, so all four are its images. That makes three checks: the epipolar constraint for m1 and m2, two trilinearities for images 1, 2 and 3, and two for images 2, 3 and 4, or in practice the distance from m4 to the point transferred from m2 and m3. Four views have tensors of their own, 81 numbers with 16 equations, but the chain makes them unnecessary. Chains like this follow a detected object through a sequence of X-ray images, where each new image must agree with the two before it.

10Summary

The key ideas next to their NumPy equivalents. In code, the cameras are mat_a, mat_b and mat_c, or p for any one of them, the pixels are homogeneous vectors such as m1 = np.array([x1, y1, 1]), and mat_q and mat_r are Q and r.
